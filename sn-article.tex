\abstract{%
Hydrogen embrittlement (HE) causes steel pipes to fracture at lower strains and with less warning than unexposed steel, yet experimental data on HE are limited and fracture models often require the crack path to be specified in advance. We pose the matching of experimental tensile stress--strain curves as an inverse problem for a state-based peridynamic correspondence model with Voce hardening and Brozzo ductile damage, and use it to learn material properties of TBF1200 steel at three hydrogen concentrations. The parameters are calibrated with Dakota in two stages, hardening first and damage second. The calibrated model reproduces the experimental fracture strains to within 0.5 percentage points. Embrittlement is captured almost entirely by a single parameter governing the onset of bond weakening, which falls from 0.85 to 0.19 as hydrogen concentration increases, while the damage-rate parameters are unchanged. We then apply the calibrated parameters, unmodified, to a pipe fragmenting under internal pressure, with no prescribed fracture path. As hydrogen concentration increases, failure begins earlier and at lower plastic strain, with the mean plastic strain at failure decreasing from 0.214 to 0.141, and the pipe absorbs less energy through plastic deformation before it fragments. These results show that peridynamic inverse calibration can extend limited tensile data to predict how hydrogen affects the failure of a pipe.}


\keywords{Peridynamics, Hydrogen embrittlement, Steel pipes, Fracture, Calibration}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Hydrogen embrittlement (HE) is the loss of ductility and toughness that occurs when hydrogen enters a metal. It is a concern for high-strength steels in service, and most often it is encountered in pipes and pressure vessels, so predicting how a pressurized steel pipe fails as its hydrogen content increases is of practical interest~\cite{chowdhury2025review}. At the microstructural level, hydrogen penetrating the steel creates voids; under load, these voids act as stress concentrations that nucleate cracks~\cite{robertson2015hydrogen}. At the structural scale, the consequence is a transition from stable, ductile deformation to early and abrupt fracture.

Experimental data on steel with different hydrogen concentrations make this transition visible in the tensile response. In the tests of Nieto et al.~\cite{nieto2024thesis} on TBF1200 sheet steel, which we use here, the stress--strain curve of the uncharged steel softens visibly over several percent strain before fracture. As the hydrogen concentration increases, this softening disappears and the material stays near its peak stress until it fractures abruptly at a much lower strain. Capturing this change in behavior, rather than only the drop in fracture strain, is a stringent test of any model of HE. Experimental data of this kind are limited, however. Only a few hydrogen concentrations have been tested, and they cannot cover the range of conditions in which HE matters in practice, including the geometry and loading of a pipe.

The harder question is how to use such limited, material-level data to predict HE in a pipe. Fracture models that represent cracks explicitly typically require the crack path to be specified, or a crack to be seeded, before the simulation begins. For a pressurized pipe this is a serious limitation, because the fracture pattern is itself the result we are after and it is not known in advance. Phase-field models avoid this requirement and have been applied to hydrogen-assisted cracking~\cite{martinezpaneda2018phasefield,navidtehrani2025generalised}, but they introduce an additional field that must be solved for alongside the displacement. Peridynamics~\cite{silling2000reformulation,silling2005meshfree,silling2007states} offers another alternative. It is a nonlocal reformulation of continuum mechanics in which each point interacts with its neighbors within a finite horizon through integral equations, rather than through spatial derivatives. Damage is represented by bond failure, so cracks can nucleate, branch, and coalesce without a prescribed path and without additional fields to track the crack. These properties make peridynamics well suited to fracture and fragmentation problems like the one considered here.

Peridynamics has previously been applied to hydrogen embrittlement, primarily by modeling the diffusion of hydrogen through a material and the cracking that results from the embrittlement it causes~\cite{demeo2016stress,ran2022crack,chen2023peridynamic}. For example, Chen et al.~\cite{chen2023peridynamic} use a two-parameter bond-based model with a hydrogen-dependent bond-failure criterion. Such a simplified bond-level constitutive description may limit the representation of plastic hardening and ductile damage that can be important in structural failure. Our approach addresses a different problem: we use a state-based correspondence model with an elastoplastic hardening law and ductile damage, calibrated to experimental stress--strain data at each hydrogen condition. We do not model hydrogen transport; instead, we use the calibrated material properties to simulate pipe failure.

These limited data motivate the approach taken here. Rather than using the stress--strain curves only to describe the specimens that were tested, we use them as ground truth for an inverse problem within a peridynamic framework: we seek the parameters of a state-based peridynamic correspondence material model, which allows a standard local constitutive law (here, Voce hardening) to be used within peridynamics, together with the Brozzo ductile damage law \cite{behzadinasab2020revisiting}, that reproduce the measured tensile response of Nieto et al.~\cite{nieto2024thesis} at each hydrogen concentration. The inverse problem is solved with Dakota~\cite{dakota2025}. Learning the material properties in this way extends the reach of the limited HE data. The calibrated parameters describe the material, not a single test, so they can be used to simulate configurations that were not tested and to assess the performance of pipes that have been exposed to hydrogen. We demonstrate this by applying the calibrated parameters, unmodified, to a fragmenting pipe loaded by internal pressure, and examining how the onset of fracture, the plastic strain at failure, and the distribution of damage change with hydrogen concentration. The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a two-stage calibration procedure that separates hardening from damage, and shows that the Brozzo damage rates transfer unchanged between hydrogen concentrations while the bond-weakening onset parameter tracks embrittlement;
\item a demonstration that parameters learned from a limited set of tensile tests can be used, without recalibration, to simulate a fragmenting pipe in which the fracture path is not prescribed; and
\item a quantitative comparison of how pipe failure changes with hydrogen concentration, in terms of failure time, plastic strain at failure, and accumulated damage.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec2} describes the peridynamic model, the hydrogen concentration cases, the calibration procedure, and the pipe simulation. Section~\ref{sec5} presents the calibration results and the pipe simulations, and Section~\ref{sec7} concludes.

\section{Methods}\label{sec2}

Material parameters governing hardening and ductile damage were calibrated against experimental
engineering stress--strain curves from slow-rate dogbone tensile tests at several hydrogen
concentrations, using peridynamic simulations of the tensile test posed as an inverse problem and
solved with Dakota. The calibrated parameters for each concentration were then used, unmodified, to
simulate a pressurized cylinder and study how the resulting damage and fragmentation behavior
changes with embrittlement level.

\subsection{Peridynamic formulation}\label{subsec2a}

Simulations were performed in Peridigm~\cite{peridigm} using a state-based peridynamic~\cite{silling2007states} hypoelastic correspondence
material model with isotropic hardening, in which the local deformation gradient is reconstructed
from the relative motion of points within a finite horizon and used to evaluate a standard
(local-form) constitutive response. The numerical formulation and implementation follow the semi-Lagrangian constitutive correspondence framework described by Behzadinasab and Foster~\cite{behzadinasab2020semilagrangian}. Hardening followed a Voce-type saturation law~\cite{voce1948}, augmented by a linear hardening term,
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_y(\varepsilon_p) = \sigma_0 + (\sigma_{\mathrm{sat}} - \sigma_0)\left(1 - e^{-n\varepsilon_p}\right) + H\varepsilon_p,
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_p$ is the equivalent plastic strain and $\sigma_0$ (Initial Yield Stress),
$\sigma_{\mathrm{sat}}$ (Saturated Yield Stress), $n$ (Exponential Constant) and $H$ (Linear
Constant) are calibrated. $H$ is a plastic hardening modulus, active only after yield, and is
distinct from the material's elastic moduli (bulk modulus 130~GPa, shear modulus 78~GPa), which were
fixed at values representative of the base TBF1200 steel and not calibrated.  The corresponding
Young's modulus, $E = 9KG/(3K+G) \approx 195$~GPa, agrees with the measured value of 201~GPa
reported for this steel~\cite{nieto2024thesis} to within 3\%.

Ductile damage was modeled with the Brozzo/Wellman damage law of Behzadinasab and Foster~\cite{behzadinasab2020revisiting}, in which
bond-level damage accumulates at a rate
\begin{equation}
  \dot D = d_1 \left\langle \frac{2\sigma_T}{3(\sigma_T - \sigma_M)} \right\rangle^{d_2} \dot{\bar\varepsilon}_p,
\end{equation}
driven by the bond-level maximum principal stress $\sigma_T$ and hydrostatic stress $\sigma_M$, and
Heaviside-gated to tension ($\langle\cdot\rangle$) so that damage accumulates only under tensile
triaxiality. A bond retains its full force contribution until its accumulated damage $D$ exceeds
the onset value $D_\mathrm{onset}$. Beyond this value, its force contribution decreases linearly with
further damage accumulation, reaching zero (full bond failure) at $D=1$. Point-level damage, reported in the Results, is the
neighborhood-volume-weighted average of this bond-level quantity over a point's horizon. The horizon
used for the dogbone specimen was 0.236~cm for neighbor search, with an actual horizon of 0.189~cm
used internally by the correspondence material's gradient/shape-tensor calculation, set empirically
rather than from a fixed rule.

\subsection{Hydrogen concentration cases}\label{subsec2b}

The tensile stress-strain data used to calibrate the model, for all three hydrogen concentrations,
were taken from the experimental work of Nieto et al.~\cite{nieto2024thesis}, who charged TBF1200
sheet steel specimens with hydrogen and measured the resulting tensile response. Three cases were
studied, denoted HE$_{0}$, HE$_{30}$, and HE$_{60}$, where the subscript is the duration,
in minutes, of electrolytic hydrogen charging (condition A30) applied to the TBF1200 sheet steel
specimen before testing (HE$_{0}$ being an uncharged baseline). Measured bulk hydrogen concentration
increases monotonically with charging time: 0, 0.68 and 1.16~mppm for HE$_{0}$, HE$_{30}$, and
HE$_{60}$ respectively~\cite{nieto2024thesis}.

Experimental fracture strain decreases monotonically with increasing concentration (15.37\%,
13.27\% and 7.68\% for HE$_{0}$, HE$_{30}$, and HE$_{60}$ respectively), and the
character of the pre-fracture curve changes along with it: at low concentration the material
softens visibly over several percent strain before fracturing, while at high concentration the
material remains near peak stress until a comparatively abrupt final failure.

\subsection{Calibration against tensile data}\label{subsec2c}

The overall calibration approach follows Behzadinasab and Foster's revisiting of the third Sandia Fracture Challenge~\cite{behzadinasab2020revisiting}: material parameters are identified from calibration tests and then applied to a different geometry. Here, the calibration is adapted to tensile data at each hydrogen condition, using the two-stage procedure described below.

The dogbone specimen was modeled with a prescribed-displacement boundary condition at each grip,
scaled by each node's own distance from the specimen center so that grip motion is affine rather
than rigid: each grip node's $y$-displacement was prescribed as $u_y = y \cdot \dot{e} \cdot t$, with
elongation-rate constant $\dot{e} = 45.0~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, common to all concentrations, run to a
final time of 1.84~ms. At the grip node sets (located at $y \in [4.70, 5.08]$~cm and $y \in
[-5.08, -4.70]$~cm), this gives nominal grip velocities of roughly 211--229~cm/s. The reported
engineering stress and strain were not read directly from this boundary displacement, but from two
tracked points at $y = \pm 1.238$~cm (gauge length 2.476~cm): strain is their relative displacement
divided by the gauge length, and stress is the grip reaction force divided by the original gauge
cross-section ($0.635 \times 0.315~\mathrm{cm}^2 = 0.200~\mathrm{cm}^2$), so that the localized
necking within the gauge section is reflected rather than averaged out over the whole specimen. A
finite horizon (as above) was used throughout. Simulations were explicit-dynamics, integrated with
Peridigm's Verlet time-stepper, and solved in parallel across four MPI ranks.
Although the tensile experiments were conducted quasi-statically, the simulations were loaded much
more rapidly to reduce the computational cost of explicit time integration during calibration.
The hardening and damage laws do not include an explicit strain-rate dependence, so this acceleration
does not directly change the constitutive response at a given deformation state. It can, however,
introduce inertial effects and small dynamic oscillations in the simulated response; the oscillatory
noise was reduced by smoothing the simulated stress--strain curves before calibration, as described
below.

Calibration was driven by Dakota~\cite{dakota2025}, an open-source toolkit developed at Sandia
National Laboratories that couples an external simulation code to a library of optimization,
parameter-estimation, uncertainty-quantification and sensitivity-analysis methods. Dakota treats the
simulation as a black box: it proposes a set of parameter values, writes them to an input file,
launches the forward simulation (here, the Peridigm dogbone model), reads back the resulting response,
and uses it to propose the next set of parameters. In this way Dakota acts as the driver of the
optimization loop, while Peridigm supplies the forward solve at each iteration. Specifically, we used
Dakota's \texttt{coliny\_pattern\_search} algorithm, a local, derivative-free direct search. The objective function was defined as the mean relative error between simulated and experimental stress.
Simulated stress--strain curves were interpolated at the experimental strain data points to evaluate the mean relative error.  Prior to the comparison, smoothing was applied to the simulated curve to reduce noise resulting from the dynamic loading nature of the simulation. Experimental points below 1\% of the curve's peak stress were
excluded, since near-zero-stress sensor noise would otherwise make the relative error unstable. Where damage was active, a
fracture-strain term (the relative error between simulated and experimental fracture strain) was
added to the stress term, and the stress comparison was restricted to strains at or below the
simulated fracture strain, since the two halves of a fractured specimen no longer produce physically
meaningful stress readings. Convergence at each stage was assessed by confirming that perturbing
each active parameter away from the current best point, in multiple directions, consistently failed
to further reduce the objective.  The resulting fit was also inspected qualitatively against the
experimental curve before advancing to the next stage.

Calibration was performed in two stages. In Stage~1, only the hardening parameters
($\sigma_0$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{sat}}$, $n$, $H$) were treated as free. Damage was fixed so that it never triggered, using a high bond-weakening onset value and a damage-rate constant well above any plastic strain reached in this stage. The fit therefore reflected hardening alone. The fracture-strain
term was also dropped from the objective at this stage.

For every concentration after HE$_{0}$, Stage~1 was warm-started from the nearest calibrated
concentration. We expected hardening to transfer well across embrittlement level, and this was
confirmed for all three concentrations. Initial Yield Stress was the only Stage~1 parameter that moved
meaningfully across concentrations. Saturated Yield Stress, the Exponential Constant and the Linear
Constant were essentially unchanged.


In Stage~2, the hardening parameters were held fixed while the Brozzo damage parameters were calibrated. The onset parameter $D_\mathrm{onset}$ was swept with $d_1$ and $d_2$ fixed; for concentrations after HE$_{0}$, their values were transferred from the previously calibrated concentration. Across concentrations, $d_1$ and $d_2$ remained essentially unchanged, whereas $D_\mathrm{onset}$ varied with embrittlement level. For HE$_{30}$, a subsequent joint search over all three damage parameters yielded a modest improvement, and those values are reported. For HE$_{0}$, $d_1$ and $d_2$ were jointly optimized with $D_\mathrm{onset}$ fixed. For HE$_{60}$, the reported values come from the onset sweep because the joint damage-parameter search did not converge. Hardening and damage parameters were not jointly re-optimized for any concentration.

\section{Results}\label{sec5}

\subsection{Dogbone calibration}\label{subsec5a}
Calibrated parameters for the three hydrogen concentrations are listed in Table~\ref{tab:calibration_stages}, and the resulting simulated stress--strain curves are compared with the experimental data in Fig.~\ref{fig:stress_strain_fit}. In Stage~1, hardening was nearly independent of hydrogen concentration. Only the initial yield stress changed: for HE$_{30}$ it was 1,027~MPa, about 5\% higher than the 977~MPa found for HE$_{0}$ and HE$_{60}$. In Stage~2, embrittlement was captured almost entirely by the bond-weakening onset value $D_\mathrm{onset}$, with $d_1$ and $d_2$ unchanged. $D_\mathrm{onset}$ falls by 40\% from HE$_{0}$ to HE$_{30}$ (0.85 to 0.51) and is 0.19 for HE$_{60}$, less than a quarter of the HE$_{0}$ value. This reduction causes the HE$_{60}$ specimen to fracture at about half the strain of HE$_{0}$ (7.80\% vs.\ 15.87\%). Figure~\ref{fig:fracture_strain_trend} shows that the simulated fracture strain decreases with hydrogen concentration and agrees with experiment to within 0.5 percentage points for all three cases, which demonstrates the embrittlement captured by the model. These parameters were then applied, unmodified, to the pressurized cylinder.

\subsection{Fragmenting pipe}\label{subsec5c}
Figure~\ref{fig:cylinder_damage_HE} shows damage in the pressurized cylinders at three times for each hydrogen concentration. The fragmentation pattern is nearly identical across the three cylinders because the same heterogeneity, added to mimic the heterogeneous microstructure of metals, is imposed in each case. Without it, cracks would not nucleate naturally. The differences between concentrations therefore lie in when cracks initiate and in how much damage accumulates within the fragments.

These snapshots are shown at later times (up to $386.7~\mu\mathrm{s}$) than the quantitative damage
statistics in Figs.~\ref{fig:eqps_at_failure} and~\ref{fig:damage_classes} (capped at
$331~\mu\mathrm{s}$), since observing the fragmentation process itself, rather than only the damage
state leading up to it, requires times past initial wall breach. The applied pressure continues to
ramp on its prescribed schedule even after the wall breaches, with no venting or depressurization
coupling, so the loading magnitude at these later times likely runs higher than a real depressurizing
pipe would experience.  This does not affect the qualitative crack-initiation-timing comparison
between concentrations shown here, which is established by material behavior before breach, but is
why quantitative statistics elsewhere are only reported up to $331~\mu\mathrm{s}$.

At the earliest time shown ($t=380.8~\mu\mathrm{s}$, top row), only the HE$_{60}$ cylinder contains points with damage above 0.5 (warm colors), and these are spread along its length. Cracks initiate at these locations, as seen in the growing gaps between fragments in the subsequent frames. In the HE$_{30}$ cylinder, fragmentation first appears at the second time ($t=384.7~\mu\mathrm{s}$), with gaps forming between fragments by the third. In the HE$_{0}$ cylinder, cracks are clearly visible only at the third time ($t=386.7~\mu\mathrm{s}$), and the gaps between fragments are much smaller than in the other two. The HE$_{0}$ steel can strain plastically and absorb energy, which delays fracture and fragmentation, whereas the embrittled HE$_{60}$ steel fractures at lower plastic strain and therefore fragments sooner.

The damage within the fragments also differs between concentrations. Fragments of the HE$_{60}$ cylinder are mostly blue, indicating little accumulated damage, whereas fragments of the HE$_{0}$ cylinder are largely warm-colored. The uncharged HE$_{0}$ steel strains plastically as it is loaded, which spreads the load over more points and raises their damage, but this plastic deformation also absorbs energy that would otherwise drive fracture. As a result, HE$_{0}$ contains many points with damage above 0.5 and yet fractures later than the embrittled cylinders.

The plastic strain accumulated by each point at the moment it fails quantifies this trend. As shown by the cumulative distributions in Fig.~\ref{fig:eqps_at_failure}, points in the more heavily charged cylinders fail at progressively lower plastic strain.  This is reflected in the mean plastic strain at failure, which decreases from 0.214 for HE$_{0}$ to 0.176 for HE$_{30}$ and 0.141 for HE$_{60}$. Figure~\ref{fig:damage_classes} shows the distribution of point damage at 331~$\mu$s. In HE$_{60}$, 49\% of points have a damage value of 0.49 or less, compared with none in HE$_{0}$. Together, these results show that as hydrogen concentration increases, the pipe's capacity to deform plastically and absorb energy decreases, so fracture initiates earlier in time and at lower plastic strain.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec7}
We used a state-based peridynamic correspondence model to learn the properties of TBF1200 steel at three hydrogen concentrations from experimental tensile curves, and then used those properties to simulate a pipe fragmenting under internal pressure. The calibrated model reproduced the experimental fracture strains to within 0.5 percentage points, and embrittlement was captured almost entirely by the bond-weakening onset value $D_\mathrm{onset}$, which decreased from 0.85 to 0.51 to 0.19 while $d_1$ and $d_2$ did not change.  The results demonstrate that the model can effectively capture the effects of hydrogen on the mechanical behavior of the material. Applied unmodified to the pipe, with no prescribed crack path, the calibrated parameters produced earlier failure at lower plastic strain as hydrogen concentration increased, with the mean plastic strain at failure decreasing from 0.214 to 0.141. The inverse problem thus extends a small set of tensile tests to a configuration that was not tested.
